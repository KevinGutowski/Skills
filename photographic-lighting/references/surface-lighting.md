## Matte And Textured

Direct reflections scatter, so the viewer reads shape from tonal variation and texture from micro-shadows. Shadow control is the whole job.

If a textured subject looks flat:

- Move the key farther off camera axis.
- Reduce fill.
- Make the source smaller or move it farther away.
- Lower the light until surface relief casts visible micro-shadows.

If a textured subject looks too harsh:

- Enlarge the source or bring it closer.
- Add fill from the shadow side.
- Rotate the subject so the strongest texture no longer runs perpendicular to the key.

## Metal

Workflow:

1. Lock the camera position first; the family of angles depends on camera-subject geometry and moves when the camera does.
2. Decide the metal tone: bright, dark, gradient, or a controlled stripe.
3. Map the family of angles (mirror test) and place the bright card or the dark surround accordingly.
4. Light the card or panel, not the metal. Large cards, panels, and diffusion surfaces are the reflected objects; do not rely on direct beams alone.
5. For a gradient, light the panel unevenly (feather the light across it); the metal shows the panel's falloff.

Curved and round metal: every visible part faces a different angle, so there are many families of angles and the metal sees most of the room. Use broad panels or long strips, and make everything else it can see intentional (black cloth, tent, or flags).

## Glass And Liquids

Front-surface light on glass gives hot spots without defining the object. Edge definition comes first.

| Scheme | Where the light goes | Edges |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bright-field | Bright background filling the frame; nothing bright at the edge-defining limits of the family of angles | Dark |
| Dark-field | Dark background filling the frame; light placed at those edge-defining limits | Bright |

- Set the background and edge relationship before adding props.
- If the glass disappears, increase edge contrast. Front light does not help.
- If a reflection distracts, move the source, camera, or card out of the family of angles, or flag it.
- A label or liquid that must read is a second subject with its own exposure and reflection problem; light it separately after the edges work.

## White-On-White And Black-On-Black

Beyond the separation rule in the body:

- **White-on-white:** a true white background with a slightly darker subject still reads as white-on-white. A gray background reads as white-on-gray, so do not let the background fall that far.
- **Black-on-black:** keep fill low enough that the scene still reads black. The edge comes from a rim or from something bright placed where the black surface reflects it, not from general fill.

Solve edge separation, then tune texture, mood, and exact tone.
