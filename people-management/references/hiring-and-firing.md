## Hiring is your job, and it's a funnel (Zhuo)

- The priority reframe — Chris Cox to Zhuo, who never let a day pass without working on recruiting while projects stalled anyway: "If I told you that hiring well was the only thing that mattered, would you do anything differently?" Of course yes — dozens of new moves appeared (referral asks, candidate coffees, pitch-honing); the four months that followed filled every open leadership role. When the team is growing, run that thought experiment for real.
- **Funnel math kills hiring anxiety.** Individual candidates are unpredictable; aggregates aren't. If one accept = two offers = eight interviews = twenty initial meetings = forty emails, then twenty hires ≈ "you'll need to send about 800 emails over the course of the year." Divide by managers (8 managers → ~4 emails/week each), set weekly interview targets, train enough interviewers, and optimize funnel stages (better first email, events, sharper questions). "Your success depends on how well you operate."
- Hiring is not delegable to recruiting: "No recruiter can possibly know what an ideal candidate looks like for *your* team." Recruiter brings network + process; you bring role understanding + personal connection with candidates.
- Plan the team annually: future org chart, gap analysis, open-roles list — so hunger doesn't make pickles-and-ketchup look like dinner.
- Leadership hires: disproportionate blast radius; research the bar by interviewing people who *hold* the role even if they'd never take yours.
- At scale, make hiring everyone's job and culture-set it: introduce every new hire at team meetings; Zhuo's proudest moment was scanning a room of great new faces and not having hired a single one of them.

## Decision rules: weak hires, advocates, pre-debrief write-ups

- **Pre-debrief independent write-ups**: "each person should independently record their rationale and their final 'hire' or 'no hire' decision before hearing other interviewers' thoughts to ensure that the discussion doesn't lead to groupthink."
- **Reject weak hires.** The unanimous-but-tepid "hire" ("I could see him being valuable on someone *else's* team," "I don't see a reason why we *shouldn't*…") is a trap: "Since every hire is already a gamble, reject any weak hires."
- **Bet on passionate advocates over consensus**: mixed reviews where the yes-votes are *adamant* about wanting to work with her usually signal something highly valued. Polarized-with-champions beats unanimous-and-lukewarm.

## Declined offers are pipeline

Top people decline; months or years later the email arrives out of the blue: the situation has changed — "Are there any opportunities on my team?" "Jobs may be short, but careers are long." Many of Zhuo's current leaders said no once or twice first. So: tell decliners you hope your paths cross again, track rising stars, build the team's external reputation — recruiting top talent is a relationships business, not a transactions business.

## Recruiting outreach and interview signals (Soleio; Ruiz)

- **"Who made that?" outreach** (Soleio, Dive Club): "I love answering the question of like well who made that there's somebody out there who designed that I'm going to go and find that person" — source candidates from artifacts you admire, not just inbound. Standing practice (Soleio, Design MBA): "some people like to play Wordle I like to just like lurk on the internet and find incredible portfolios… I'm just a talent spotter."
- **Recruiting is a long game** (Soleio, Dive Club): "even if they don't join you in year one… you want to be top of their list in year three or year five" (Adam Mosseri re-entered Facebook's design pipeline by shipping a hit platform app, Boombox). Pairs with Zhuo's declined-offers-are-pipeline above.
- **Recruiting is the most-leveraged design activity** (Soleio, Design MBA): "that was the most leverag thing that I can do at the company" [leveraged]. Keep the CEO in the close — Drew Houston "never backed off from helping out with closing candidates… they want to hear directly from The Keeper of the vision and the strategy."
- **Interview for slope: time-to-proficiency** (Soleio, Dive Club): "what is it time to proficiency name a thing you're proficient at when did you start learning" — "sometimes you'll find slope just in the simple question of like you're really good at guitar like when did you start playing Guitar." Because "one's ability to learn new things quickly is like a meta skill in and of itself and then ask the question of uh you know what are you learning now where are you early in the curve over the next 2 years what do you wish to learn." Watch for horizontal movers — "moving through their careers horizontally across disciplines rather than like going depthwise into a particular field sort of going straight into Mastery mode perhaps prematurely" (Lee Byron: data scientist → product designer → co-creator of React/GraphQL).
- **Hire one profile across all roles** (Ruiz, SPC fireside — Steve Ruiz, tldraw; Soleio moderated): "I I try and hire like the same person, the same type of profile across all roles" — "generally full stack," someone "who like spikes in in in various places," with "Open source maintainer being like the best kind of profile": a person who "had to like triage issues that they didn't create themselves about something that was still theirs. But but someone who had to make the logo for the thing."

## Scott's committee mechanics

- Hiring manager (not a recruiter) writes the job description, defining team **fit** as rigorously as skills — three or four culture words ("detail-oriented, quirky, blunt") interviewed for deliberately, which crowds out gut-call bias. Define the growth trajectory the role needs (rock star vs superstar) too.
- Blind skills assessments prescreen: work-sample tasks, identity stripped where possible (the orchestra blind-audition effect).
- **Committee of ~four, consistent across candidates** (different panels per candidate make results incomparable), diverse, **with at least one interviewer from another team — this prevents "desperation hiring"**: someone who isn't feeling the pain of the hole on the team is the one who'll point out the warning signs everyone else is ignoring.
- Manufacture **casual moments**: lunch, and "An executive I know who's a great hirer always walks candidates to the car" — unguarded minutes that surfaced a drug habit, a my-women-in-line braggart, and a gossip. Ask the receptionist and scheduler for reactions (Scott no-hired a strong candidate who made the scheduler cry; hired the one who picked up napkins he hadn't dropped).
- Write feedback immediately — "schedule an hour, interview for forty-five minutes, and write for fifteen." Writing is where Scott caught the charming candidate whose "scale to infinity" was a few million rows.
- **In-person debrief with study hall**: one-hour meeting, first fifteen minutes silent reading of everyone's feedback, then discuss; ends early when run well.
- The bar: "if you're not dying to hire somebody, don't make an offer" — and even if you are dying to, let any strong objector overrule you. "A bias toward no is useful when hiring."
- Promotions, same fairness machinery: Google engineering's twice-yearly off-site committees debated *other* managers' reports from objective packets — the fix for favoritism and backroom my-person/your-person bartering.

## Firing: the 3-questions test

Is it time to fire "Peggy"? Three questions (Scott):
1. **Have you given Radically Candid guidance** — caring personally, challenging directly, specifically, repeatedly over time — and seen no real improvement?
2. **Do you understand the impact on her colleagues?** By the time poor performance reaches you, it's usually been driving peers nuts for a long time.
3. **Have you sought a second opinion** — someone trusted, plus HR/legal reality (have an experienced person *edit* your PIP and emails, not just advise: managers hear "fair" and ignore "not too easy").

Identify underperformance *early* — in Scott's classes nearly everyone says the company has underperformers and almost no one has put a name in a poor-performance box. Early identification is fairness four ways: to the person (time to improve, less shock), to the company (legal risk), to you (a good rating one quarter and a firing the next destroys everyone's trust), and above all to the people performing well.

## The four lies managers tell themselves

1. **"It will get better."** It won't by itself. What exactly will you do differently? What will they do differently? No precise answer → it won't get better.
2. **"Somebody is better than nobody."** Poor performers create as much extra work as they accomplish. Steve Jobs, succinctly if harshly: "It's better to have a hole than an asshole."
3. **"A transfer is the answer."** Passing them to an unsuspecting colleague feels "nicer" and is a mistake for everyone, including the person.
4. **"It's bad for morale."** Keeping someone who can't do the job is far worse for morale — yours, theirs, and every strong performer carrying the slack. (Scott: morale has always *improved* after removing a poor performer; she's lost the people she most wanted to keep by hanging on too long. Retained bad *bosses* are the most damaging case.)

## How to fire with humanity

- Reframe before the meeting: "it's not the person who sucks, it's the job that sucks—at least for this person." Imagine specifically what job *would* be great for them; offer introductions if you can.
- Don't distance yourself; do it in the way easiest on *them* even if harder on you — Scott overruled her lawyer's security-guard advice and let the man say goodbye on his own terms; he thanked her later.
- **Follow up about a month later.** Scott emails every fired person ~a month out, keeps an ear open for fitting jobs, and doesn't push if they don't reply. One man (and his wife) thanked her profusely — leaving was good for his career, his marriage, and his kids.
- Companies where firing is too *easy* breed fear and risk-aversion; too *hard* breeds foisted transfers and "the gravitational pull of organizational mediocrity." Calibrate against both.

## Open-call hiring without a recruiting machine (37signals)

Zhuo's funnel math assumes an outbound machine and a recruiting org. The 37signals shape is the opposite — a public call that draws thousands of applications, with a small HR team owning screening and candidate communication while the hiring manager appears only at defined points. Useful when you have one role, no sourcing budget, and a real audience.

- **"We hire when it hurts."** Absent a backfill, the decision comes from the hiring team's consensus "after feeling the pain of lack of momentum for a sustained period of time," and is approved by leadership before anything is posted. Pain first, headcount second.
- **Rewrite the job post every time.** Past ads are inspiration, "not simple copy paste" — each round updates examples of current work, quality standards, and an accurate description of the process the candidate is about to enter.
- **Front-load the screening criteria.** Before applications arrive, brief whoever screens on hard skills, expertise level, past experience, and how you want applications and cover letters to present work. "Give the fullest, most ideal picture of a candidate you can" — screening quality is capped by the specificity of this briefing.
- **Three buckets, not a score:** *Disqualified* (little or no criteria met), *Set Aside* (meets criteria, quality application, look again), *Closer Review* (put in front of the hiring manager).
- **Cap what the manager reviews** — no more than 50 Closer Review candidates, star ratings plus optional comments, and explicitly **no quota**: "pass or fail based purely on merit." Roughly 5–15 then move to screening calls.
- **Screens are for blockers and humanity**, not skill: introduce the company and process, humanize it, surface visa issues, application inconsistencies, or personality problems. The manager approves the template/rubric and reads notes as they land, adding flags the screener wouldn't catch. Expect the pool to halve.
- **Then the work sample** (see below), then interviews with the manager plus one teammate, centred on the candidate's own project: technical choices, how they approached the problem, how they communicate about it.
- **Three legitimate outcomes**, and the third is not a failure: make an offer · run follow-up interviews because you still have questions · hire nobody. "As disappointing as it can be to not hire anyone, making that call during the hiring process is far preferable to hiring with uncertainty, hiring someone that only adds drag to you and your team, or, worst case, needing to fire a colleague shortly after hiring them." Same conclusion as Scott's "bias toward no," reached from the other end of the funnel.
- Keep strong non-hires' materials for the next round, and hand the accepted offer straight into an onboarding plan → [performance-cadence.md](performance-cadence.md).

## Designing the take-home exercise (37signals)

The hiring manager designs it. Three constraints, all about validity:

1. **It resembles real work** — "similar to something we'd ask an employee to work on during a typical cycle."
2. **It forces the crucial skills** to the surface — the ones that actually decide success in the job (e.g. HTML/CSS for a designer), not adjacent ones.
3. **It leaves room to explore** — you are evaluating "not just their technical work but also how they approach the problem to be solved and how they communicate their ideas," which a tightly-specified task destroys.

Mechanics: state a deadline and submission instructions in the brief; candidates decide how much time to spend (a couple of weeks of calendar); grade pass/fail before interviewing; interview the passes about their own submission.

**Pressure-test the brief with AI before sending it:** ask it to find ambiguities that would trip candidates, to judge whether the scope matches the seniority you're hiring, to check that the skills you care about are actually assessed, and to attempt the exercise as a candidate would so you can see what a mediocre-but-plausible response looks like. Limits on AI in people decisions → [performance-cadence.md](performance-cadence.md).

## The underperformance path, step by step (37signals)

Scott's three-questions test decides *whether*. This is the *how*, with the escalation order that most first-time managers get backwards.

**Definition of severe:** falling below the written expectations for the person's job and level — or "a particularly crucial one-time mistake that severely impacted operations." Unsure whether it qualifies? "Most often you should err on the side of caution and intervene."

1. **Identify the lapse and classify it.** Pattern or one-time mistake? "A one-time mistake earns more leniency than a pattern."
2. **Escalate first, talk second.** Contact HR "as soon as you've identified a serious performance lag, and before you begin talking to your report about correcting it so you can align with People Ops on the path forward." Improvising a correction plan and then telling HR is the common inversion; it produces plans that can't be honoured.
3. **Design the plan together with HR:** a correction path with a timeline and *measurable* benchmarks. The timeline is deliberately movable in one direction — "if your report fails to show improvement after a few weeks, you may proceed directly to termination."
4. **Deliver it unambiguously.** Direct feedback on what went wrong, what must change, by when, and that this is not a routine corrective chat: their job is in jeopardy and they need to recover "with urgency." Offer support in the same breath.
5. **Work the plan.** Weekly HR check-ins, your candid running impressions, and an obligation to say so early if you see no path to recovery.
6. **Commit to a firm decision** — retain or terminate — on the date you set. "If you cannot do that," leadership decides with you.
7. **Termination logistics** are mostly HR's; the manager owns two items: when it happens, and scheduling the conversation.
8. **After the call, you're done.** HR handles last-day logistics and paperwork.

When this workload starts eating your own core job, hand off performance management rather than absorbing it → [authority-and-escalation.md](authority-and-escalation.md).

## Recovering from underperformance — and graduating out of it

The step most PIP literature omits: what happens when it *works*. "Not all underperformance results in termination. When a manager identifies a performance issue early, addresses it directly, and works with their report to correct it, recovery is not only possible but common."

- **Be explicit about what improvement looks like and by when.** Vagueness here is what makes recovery feel unwinnable.
- **Check in to show investment, not surveillance** — and name progress when you see it: "a manager who only shows up to deliver bad news is not as effective as one who also shows up to say, 'I've noticed your effort and your improvement in [specific area], and it matters.'"
- **Declare the end.** When heightened monitoring is no longer needed, *tell the report* and tell HR — and celebrate it. An unannounced end leaves someone permanently on probation in their own head.
- **Expect residual doubt, and handle it with the rubric.** Trust was damaged; second-guessing your own "graduated" verdict is normal. "lean on the performance metrics. Those are objective, clear descriptions of level-appropriate performance" rather than your feelings about the last bad quarter.
- The early-warning system that makes recovery likely is the per-cycle check, not the annual review → [performance-cadence.md](performance-cadence.md).

**The authority note underneath all of this:** a manager who is also a peer has to switch modes when performance lags — "you have to assume a position of authority. That means upholding the very high standards we've benchmarked… making difficult judgment calls based on hard evidence, and standing behind those calls."

## The two hard conversations: scripts

**Preparing (either conversation).** Don't delay — "It's natural to want to avoid the issue and hope it resolves without a tough conversation. But that's unfair to your employee and to the rest of the team who are probably experiencing the repercussions." Write your talking points down first, so you don't drift into hypotheticals or get pulled into a debate. Use a human sounding board (HR or an experienced manager). AI can check whether your concerns are supported by your documentation and flag anything that reads as personal rather than professional — but never let it write the script.

**The underperformance conversation — five points to hit:**

1. Name the areas where performance is deficient, in the language of the leveling framework.
2. Cite specific examples from their work.
3. Share the impact on the team's productivity or cohesion.
4. Explain what must change for you to consider them back on trajectory.
5. State the consequence and the deadline — e.g. "I need to see improvement by the end of this cycle or we may decide to terminate your employment at that time."

Then the delivery rules: **open on your established relationship** — state positive intent and what you want out of the meeting, and ask questions that leave room to respond, rather than delivering a monologue. **Hit your points and back off**; "Hammering similar points over and over doesn't strengthen your position, only demoralizes your report." **Manage your own state** — know beforehand whether you're impatient, anxious, or insecure about the conversation, so it doesn't leak. **Workshop the path forward**: bring your firm directives and benchmarks, and still build the improvement steps jointly — "Let go of any notion that you know the best path forward, or conversely, that your report must come up with it on their own." **Document and follow up**, holding the dates you set: if a mid-cycle deliverable was the deadline, don't let it slip. Follow-up questions that check the work *and* the person: How are you progressing on [the plan]? How can I help? Any thoughts or questions now that time has passed? How are you feeling since we last talked?

Then **make the call when you said you would**, and explain the reasoning either way — including when it's going well ("stay the course").

**The termination call.** Same preparation rules, but: "it's not much of a conversation… most of the time, a termination call will last fewer than 5 minutes. Your primary job is to convey information clearly and with compassion."

- We've decided to terminate your employment/contract with the company.
- Your last day is [day].
- You're being let go because [the performance problems already discussed].
- [HR] will follow up with you on the specifics.
- Do you have anything you'd like to discuss?

The last question "is not an invitation for litigation" — if they argue, negotiate, or turn hostile, refer them to HR and end the call. Two failure modes to guard against: **rambling** ("Say what you want to say, then stop. It's easy to start rambling to fill the silence"), and **making it about you** — "avoid phrases like 'I don't know how to say this' or 'I feel terrible'. They're having a worse day than you are." Note the tension with Scott's do-it-in-the-way-easiest-on-them humanity: it isn't a contradiction. Warmth goes into *how* you deliver and what you do afterward, not into extending the call to relieve your own discomfort.
