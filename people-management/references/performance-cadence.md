# Performance Cadence

Source: *The 37signals Manager Playbook* (basecamp.com/managers, read 2026-08-13) — chapters 2, 5, 6, 7, 9 and 15, plus the AI-use passages in 4, 10 and 14. One company's operating manual, published openly. Quotes verified.

**What transfers vs what doesn't:** the *mechanisms* — a named assessment model, a fixed review calendar, per-cycle informal checks, evidence-backed write-ups, targeted peer questions — are portable to any team. The *policy specifics* (level ladder, two-year progression requirement, one-year retention bar, who owns comp) are that company's and must be replaced with your own org's before you quote them at anyone. Legal/HR-sensitive steps → [authority-and-escalation.md](authority-and-escalation.md).

## Why a cadence at all

The playbook's argument for management as a function, not a perk: "When we manage well, the business compounds. When we don't manage, the business degrades. Subtly at first, then all at once." Five compounding channels, each of which the cadence below defends:

- **Retention.** "people don't quit jobs, they quit bosses" — and backfilling costs institutional knowledge and momentum. "retention is not purely sentimental; it's necessary for operational stability."
- **Product quality.** "Standards begin to erode when you avoid clarity and expectations are unclear. Judgment declines, the boundaries of ownership and accountability blur, and unchecked apathy seeps into technical work." Quality control is a team-level job, not only an individual-level one.
- **Scale.** "If every decision flows up, you become a bottleneck." Teaching decision-making is the only way out → coaching, in [growth-and-careers.md](growth-and-careers.md).
- **Risk.** "Documenting performance concerns early and every time reduces the chance for legal or compliance problems down the line."
- **Culture.** "Values written on a page do not define culture, but the behaviors of the people within the organization do… The behaviors you as leaders reward become aspirational, and what you ignore becomes permissible." Corollary the playbook states plainly: keep your energy positive about decisions you disagreed with; visible discontent from a manager is a morale tax (the disagree-and-commit rule in [meetings-and-comms.md](meetings-and-comms.md)).

Counterweight, from the same source: "Managing people at 37signals should not take much of your time. We hire people who can operate as managers of one, so they should be free from your oversight most of the time." The cadence is deliberately light in steady state and heavy at two moments: a new hire's first year, and any lag in performance.

## SEC: the one assessment model

One framework for every role, with per-job/per-level expectations layered on top:

| Category | What it covers | Teachable? |
|---|---|---|
| **Skills** | technical ability, judgment, decision-making, problem solving, project management, communication | "Skills can be taught and improved with coaching, but they're also the most crucial" — without skill the person fails in role |
| **Engagement** | how they show up: energy, interest, initiative, curiosity, ownership, accountability, independence, attitude | "Engagement can be coached, but not taught; it's inherent." |
| **Coachability** | receptivity to feedback, growth mindset, disagree-and-commit, reputation as a source of wisdom | teachable; also the thing that makes the other two fixable |

The same model is reused at every decision point — leveling a new hire, writing a review, coaching progression, and articulating underperformance — so the language a report hears in a 1:1 is the language on their review form and their promotion case. **The portable rule: pick one vocabulary for performance and use it everywhere.** A separate rubric per occasion is how reviews become surprises.

## SEC scans (HIGH/MID/LOW) and the escalation patterns

A scan is a 30-second gut assessment on the three axes — not a substitute for a real review, but a way to notice drift between them. Expect fluctuation: "One person might be low-engagement on one project and high-engagement on the next. It's human and normal."

**Escalate any of these patterns** (in any order across the three axes): high-low-low, mid-low-low, mid-mid-low. Two lows is the signal, regardless of how high the skill score is.

The worked example is the one every strong-IC team recognizes: a brilliant senior programmer, hired for skill, who communicates with nobody, disappears for weeks, and answers concerns by citing his contributions. Scan = high-skill / low-engagement / low-coachability. The value of the scan is that it converts *he's difficult but great* into a specific, defensible statement of what must change.

## The review calendar

| Point | Required for | What "on track" means |
|---|---|---|
| **3 months** | everyone, year one | Thin evidence by design — "Work with what you have," draw conclusions about grasp of expected skills |
| **6 months** | everyone, year one | "they should be meeting most, if not all, level expectations" |
| **12 months** | everyone, year one | Meets all level expectations, ideally exceeds in a few — *plus an explicit commitment to retain* |
| **2 years** | L1→L2, L2→L3 | Progression to the next level is expected; by then, "exceeding expectations in all categories" at that level |
| **Annual** | junior through senior (L1–L3); manager discretion for lead/principal (L4–L5) | Steady state |

**The one-year bar is enthusiasm, not adequacy.** "The bar for remaining on the team at the 1-year mark isn't just passable performance, it's hell yeah! You're excited to have them on the team in their current state, and you can't wait to see what they do next." The manager then meets with HR after the review to confirm the bar was met and documented. Whatever your org calls it, the transferable move is a **named, dated, binary retain decision** early enough that a bad hire is a one-year problem, not a five-year one — and a manager who cannot say "hell yeah" at that point must escalate before the date, not after.

Process shape worth copying: report and manager fill out *separate* forms (manager assessment + employee self-reflection), HR reviews and distributes both, and both parties read them **the day before** the conversation. Nobody reads a verdict for the first time out loud.

## New-report ramp: what to actually teach in year one

Weekly 1:1s in the first months exist to transfer the things that are nowhere in the job description. Day 1 call: welcome, map the weeks ahead, name people to meet on and off the team, and set the recurring 1:1. Then work through, repeatedly, across the first cycles:

- **How we work** — tools, process, how feedback is given and received, how to flag a roadblock and ask for help, plus the local idioms (at 37signals, "scope hammering" and "judo" — every team has these, and new hires can't infer them).
- **How we communicate** — working in the open, which channel carries what, how to get feedback from senior teammates, what check-ins are for (engagement, radiating information).
- **How competency is assessed** — be explicit about which skills you expect to see immediately vs after a few cycles; walk the progression framework at 3/6/12 months using examples from their *own* cycle work.
- **The squishy categories** — mastery, judgment, decision-making, explained through your own experience. New people cannot visualize these from rubric language.
- **What's written between the lines** — a reasonable sense of urgency, shipping early, moving work forward without permission.
- **Your quality bar** — "Describe what high quality work means to you — what are you looking to see?"
- **The year-one arc** — the retention bar and how you'll take that temperature, plus level expectations (e.g. L2 in two years, L3 two years later).
- **A dedicated small first project** with a real kick-off, live feedback on current work, and the *reasoning* behind your feedback, not just the verdict.
- **Documentation duty** — which role-specific docs exist and what they're responsible for maintaining.

Two failure modes the playbook calls out: assuming this is a week-one download (it needs continual reinforcement all year), and assuming the technical ramp is the hard part — "Oftentimes getting used to how we work is the biggest struggle for new hires, more so than the technical piece." Any suspicion of lagging performance in year one is an immediate escalation, not a wait-and-see.

## Cycle reviews: the anti-coast mechanism

Between formal review points, do an informal look-back **at the end of every project cycle**, using the same standards as the formal review. This is the mechanism that keeps the gap between "1-year hell yeah" and "2-year progression" from becoming a silent year that ends in a surprise decision.

- **Time limit on benefit of the doubt:** "a cycle or two to allow someone to show development is great; six months is not."
- **Say you're doing it, and say what you found** — both directions. "If you look back and are impressed with their work, tell them. Likewise if you look back and found they needed a whole lot of help to get something over the finish line, tell them you noticed and that they need to recover from that."
- The cumulative record from these checks is what makes a later promotion case or a later termination defensible.

Underperformance that survives cycle reviews → [hiring-and-firing.md](hiring-and-firing.md).

## Writing the review: what you're weighing

Five questions, deliberately phrased as thinking prompts rather than rubric rows:

1. **Work done** — quantity *and* impact. Did they pull their weight?
2. **Skill shown** — technical proficiency, judgment, adaptability to the team's ways of working.
3. **Correction needed** — material and superficial. "If you go back and look at your report's projects, how much red pen is there? Did you provide the right amount of oversight, and how much of you or another teammate is represented in the final work product?" (This is the sharpest of the five: it measures the *hidden subsidy* a report is receiving, which no self-assessment surfaces.)
4. **Engagement** — communicative, transparent, helpful, easy to work with, "do they run towards problems?"
5. **Coachability** — do they internalize feedback and stop repeating the mistake; do they seek it and give it well?

Back every assessment with a **deep review of actual artifacts** — links to PRs, project work, documents — in the review form itself. An assessment with no receipts is a recollection.

## Peer feedback: three questions, designed

Ask the people who work most closely with the report — the design counterpart for a programmer, and so on. Ask **2–3 specific questions**, never *how's it going working with X*:

- "In the past 6 weeks, what is one specific example of when [report] made your work easier or more effective?" — *forces recency and surfaces strengths.*
- "What is 1 thing [report] could do differently that would improve your ability to work with them?" — *forces prioritization; one thing, not a list.*
- "If you were to work with [report] again next [cycle/project], what would you want them to do more of or less of?" — *forces future orientation; gets practical positive and corrective notes in one answer.*

Remind respondents to be candid. Then: **never pass negative peer feedback verbatim** — summarize it, anonymize it as far as possible. Verbatim quotes turn a review into a whodunit and poison the next collaboration.

## Goals at review time

The review is also the goal-setting moment. Have the report propose:

- **At least 2 attainable goals** that build on work already done.
- **1–2 stretch goals** that "would be impressive to achieve, but are not instrumental to team operations or output" — the definition matters: a stretch goal the team depends on is just an unstaffed commitment.

Discuss their proposals, add your input, **document the outcome**, and follow up during the year rather than rediscovering the list at the next review.

## Documentation and storage

Store review documents in two places: somewhere the *report* can reread them whenever they want, and somewhere you and HR share for notes, goals, and follow-ups. The second copy is what makes the goals real and what protects everyone if the trajectory turns. Same rule for 1:1 agreements → [meetings-and-comms.md](meetings-and-comms.md).

## The manager's own standard

The playbook applies SEC to managers themselves — a usable self-audit:

- **Skills:** you give guidance on technical approach, execution, project management, and navigating the culture; you keep visibility into performance, development, and comms; you hold consistent practices (1:1s, reviews, work reviews); you keep "accurate, comprehensive, and progressive documentation"; you set expectations *including what "exceeds expectations" looks like in practice*, and you intervene on performance issues early.
- **Engagement:** you participate in manager forums; your 1:1s hold time for development, not just status; you recognize wins publicly and privately; your feedback is prompt, specific, and *followed through to noticeable change*; your team can raise concerns and admit mistakes without fear.
- **Coachability:** you seek advice from other managers; you give leadership advice grounded in experience when asked; you actively ask reports how to support them better and respond to corrective notes "with curiosity, not defensiveness."

## AI as a prep lens (bounded)

The playbook sanctions four narrow uses, each a *critique* of work you already did — never a generator of the thing you'll say:

- **Interview exercises:** pressure-test a take-home brief for ambiguity and scope-vs-seniority fit; have the model attempt it as a candidate would → [hiring-and-firing.md](hiring-and-firing.md).
- **Annual reviews:** feed your own documented feedback, 1:1 notes, and observations from the year and ask for themes, inconsistencies, and repeated concerns you've lost sight of by being close to the work.
- **Feedback drafts:** draft your talking points, then ask what sounds vague, person-focused rather than behavior-focused, or unspecific; ask it to react as the report might, to preview how it lands.
- **Promotion:** ask it to "find the diff" between the current and next level's expectations and turn job descriptions into implied criteria, so you assess against the level gap rather than recent memory.

**Hard limits.** For high-stakes conversations: "Do not use AI to create a script; you'll sound rehearsed and run the risk of getting mired in stating your points versus having a real, two-sided conversation." AI output "should be used to support assessment work you've already done and not as a checklist or bar for promotion readiness." Two guardrails to add locally before using any of this: check what your org's privacy/HR policy allows you to paste about an identifiable employee, and treat model agreement as no evidence at all — it is a lens on *your* documentation, and it will agree with a biased draft as readily as a fair one. Standing corpus caution → [coverage-gaps.md](coverage-gaps.md).
