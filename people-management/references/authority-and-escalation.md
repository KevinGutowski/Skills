# Authority and Escalation

Source: *The 37signals Manager Playbook* (basecamp.com/managers, read 2026-08-13) — chapters 3, 9, 15. Quotes verified.

**Why this file exists:** the management books in this corpus teach what to *do* with a report. They say little about the areas where a manager's job is to stop, hand off, and not improvise — leave, complaints, pay, and legal exposure. Those are exactly the areas where a well-meaning improvised answer creates real harm. The specific policies below are one company's; the transferable artifact is a **written boundary map you build with your own HR/legal function before you need it.**

## The boundary map

Areas that "overlap or nudge up against your managerial responsibilities, but you won't be solely responsible for them." For each one, know three things cold: **what you decide, what you route, and what you must not say.** If you can't answer those for leave, complaints, and pay today, that's the first thing to fix with HR — not a thing to work out live in a 1:1.

## Time off: inform, not request

Policy: "employees inform their manager and team of their Paid Time Off (PTO) plans, they don't request PTO. The difference is subtle but important; the former is how to treat responsible, capable adults."

- **You own coverage**, not permission. Work with the report so their cycle commitments land around their plans.
- **Don't deny** absent a genuinely critical event that precludes any time off.
- **Don't ask how they plan to spend it.** (If it comes up in conversation, fine.)
- Your job is to facilitate the time off if they need help taking it, "and getting out of their way if they don't."
- Long leave (a sabbatical, in this org six weeks) gets the same posture: plan the team's coverage around it rather than negotiating whether it happens.

The portable rule survives the policy: whatever your org's approval mechanics are, treat time off as a logistics problem you solve *with* the person, not a privilege you grant.

## Leave programs: escalate, never advise

Short/long-term disability, parental leave, and jurisdiction-specific programs are benefits law, not management judgment. The playbook's instruction is blunt: "Please don't advise your report on how to use Disability Leave without consulting People Ops," and the same for state-funded programs — escalate "without advising your report on their eligibility status."

Named triggers to route immediately:

- Frequent sick time reaching the point of team disruption.
- Unexpectedly out, or planning to be out, **more than 7 consecutive work days** for illness or injury.
- Any new-child news → parental leave process starts with HR.

Saying *I think you'd qualify for X* is the failure mode. You are not the benefits administrator, and a wrong guess becomes a promise the person plans their family around.

## Complaints: the hard stop

- **Serious conflict between employees:** consult HR *immediately* on receiving the complaint. They run the investigation, hold confidentiality, take disciplinary action, and handle remediation.
- **Harassment, discrimination, retaliation, hostile work environment — or any mention that a complaint has been or may be made to a regulatory agency:** inform HR immediately **and end the conversation**, using words like: "I need to bring People Ops into this before we go any further."

That script is the whole point of the section. Continuing to gather details out of sympathy or curiosity contaminates the investigation, exposes the company and the complainant, and may itself be actionable. "Conduct complaints and allegations of labor violations are some of the most serious circumstances you can find yourself in as a manager." Rehearse the sentence so it's available under pressure.

## Compensation and profit share: no input, real responsibility

In this model managers have **no input on pay**: salaries come from an annual external benchmarking study, there's no incentive pay, and "Everyone in the same role at the same level is paid the same… regardless of location or years of work experience." Adjustments run once a year on a fixed date, and pay is never decreased when the market drops. Profit sharing is a tenure-based formula "that requires no input or interpretation from managers."

Your actual role in both:

1. **Be available for questions** when letters go out (HR briefs managers before employees receive them).
2. **Carry substantive feedback back** to HR.
3. **Restate the policy accurately** — including the uncomfortable parts. On variable pay: swings are possible, a payout may not happen at all, it should not be in anyone's household budget, and leaving — resignation or termination — forfeits it with no negotiated payout.

Generalize it as: **know exactly which compensation decisions you own.** Where you have input, say so and use it. Where you don't, say *that* plainly — never soften it into an implied promise (*I'll push for you*), and never volunteer numbers, timing, or eligibility you don't control. Promotion is a separate lever with its own case → [growth-and-careers.md](growth-and-careers.md); in orgs without merit raises it is often the *only* reward lever, which raises the stakes on getting the case right.

## Your own capacity is a boundary too

Managing someone through severe underperformance is expensive, and the playbook explicitly refuses to let it consume your individual-contributor work: "if at any point you experience a lack of capacity to perform your own core job responsibilities due to managing a report who is underperforming, alert People Ops at once." HR can and does take over performance management in those cases. "Your managerial responsibilities in cases of severe underperformance are significant, but they are also easily shed."

Two corollaries worth keeping:

- **Ask before your own performance slips**, not after. The trigger is "an inordinate amount of time" or "a distressing amount of mental energy," which you will notice weeks before it shows up in your output.
- **Delivering a termination is still normally yours.** It can be handed to HR if you genuinely cannot do it — but in this model that hand-off may cost you management duties going forward. Know your org's version of that trade before you invoke it.

## Escalation triggers

A concrete checklist to keep with your 1:1 notes. Route to HR/legal *before* the next conversation with the report when any of these fire:

- [ ] Harassment, discrimination, retaliation, hostile-environment, or regulator/agency mention → end the conversation on the spot.
- [ ] Any serious interpersonal complaint or allegation about another employee.
- [ ] Illness/injury absence past your org's threshold (7 consecutive work days here), disruptive repeated absence, or any leave-eligibility question.
- [ ] New child, medical accommodation, or anything touching a protected characteristic.
- [ ] SEC scan lands on two lows in any combination → [performance-cadence.md](performance-cadence.md).
- [ ] Severe performance lapse identified — escalate **before** you tell the report how they'll fix it, so the plan is aligned first → [hiring-and-firing.md](hiring-and-firing.md).
- [ ] You've documented the same problem more than once: "escalate issues to People Ops when you find yourself documenting problems more than once, before you reach out to your report to discuss."
- [ ] Suspected lagging performance from a first-year hire.
- [ ] Personal/health disclosures you're not equipped to handle — be empathetic, redirect to the work, and route the rest.
- [ ] Managing the situation is eating your own core work.

Local verification is mandatory before any of this becomes advice you give: employment law, leave entitlements, and investigation duties vary by jurisdiction and change. See [coverage-gaps.md](coverage-gaps.md).
