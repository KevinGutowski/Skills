## The decisive rule: facts and decisions, not synonyms

Model copy now avoids the old buzzwords and still reads as model copy, because it uses one voice for every client, the same devices in every section, sincerity stated instead of shown, and invented detail to look specific. Professional copy uses the same devices but differs in five ways: its details are true (often a little inconvenient), each device is used once where it lands hardest, every brand has its own voice, it takes a risk (names an enemy, admits a limit), and its pages are uneven, leaving out what has nothing true to say. The cause is missing input. With no facts, a model fills a template with plausible inventions, so the fix is facts and decisions. Swapping a flagged word for a synonym just produces the next tell.

## 1. Fact sheet from the brief

Before writing a sentence, record what the brief, the client's files, or the existing site give, word for word where possible. Each field feeds a part of the page:

| Field | Holds | Feeds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | One plain sentence, no adjective | Hero and sub-line |
| For whom, and not for whom | The best-fit buyer; who should look elsewhere | Hero or sub-line; one FAQ line |
| What they do today instead | A spreadsheet, a rival, nothing | The pain line, comparisons |
| The true difference | What is true here and not elsewhere, with its proof | The claims worth a heading |
| Customer words | Phrases from reviews, emails, quotes | Pain line, headings |
| Numbers | Each with its origin | Sentences, never a decorative strip |
| People | Names and roles the brief gives | About, quotes |
| Odd true details | History, an object, a ritual, a house word | The texture a model would otherwise invent |
| A limit | What it does not do, who it is wrong for | One admission per page |
| An enemy | A habit or a category, never the reader | The pain line, one section |
| The offer | Price, trial, booking terms, the one action | Buttons and the close |

- **An empty field stays empty.** Its section shrinks or goes, and the handoff lists what the client must supply. Never ship a bracket, a `[TBD]`, or "coming soon" in place of a fact. Invented proof (reviews, counts, customer names, awards) is never acceptable; see `frontend-design` (anti-slop-catalog) for the placeholder-facts rule.
- **A thin brief is not a reason to stop.** Write the best page the facts allow. When a person is present and the task is copy, ask at most about six questions in one message, most useful first: who switched to you from what and why; the one number you would bet on; the objection you hear most; what you are bad at or not for; a strange true detail of how you work; three real customer messages.
- **Limits and enemies come from the brief or plain facts of the trade** ("closed Mondays", "Mac only"). An invented flaw is still an invention.

## 2. Voice card

Write a short card in the plan, never on the page. It fixes: the register (below); two or three this-but-not-that pairs (the parent's format: "exact, not cold"); person (for example "you" for what the buyer gets, "we" for the work, the product as subject in feature lines); sentence length range with permission for one long sentence per section; where humour is allowed; house words (the brief's own nouns, the same word every time); and a short never list.

- **The client's own voice comes first.** A writing sample, an old site, or the brief's wording overrides any register menu.
- **The nine-trades test.** Picture the voice on nine businesses in nine other trades. If it suits any of them, push it further toward this client.
- **Voice holds, tone moves.** The hero and the story carry the most voice. Prices, FAQ, booking, and forms go plain, because readers there are anxious. This is the parent's "voice recedes in urgent flows" applied to one page.
- **The model's default voice is not a register.** Warm, wry, modest, a little literary, ending paragraphs on a deadpan twist and calling itself honest or calm: it is what every model writes now. Use it only when the client asks.
- **Across several pages in one session**, when nothing in the brief settles the register, pick the one least like the last page you wrote.

## 3. Registers to choose from

Choose one per client by the buyer, the price, and the client's real character; borrow at most one move from a second. Each register has a failure mode, and the failure mode is the check.

| Register | Moves | Fits | Fails when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Facts only | Plain declaratives with names, dates, counts; exclusivity shown by a fact | Professional services, B2B, consultants | The facts are weak: it reads as an empty CV |
| Technical and dry, with proof | A label paired with a hard figure; the mechanism in verb-led steps; real commands and UI strings | Developer tools, infrastructure, APIs | It becomes a spec dump with no pain named |
| Founder's letter | First person; a named habit as the enemy; one argument carried across sections | Founder-led products with a stance | The stance is put on |
| Self-aware and playful | Straight face about a small product; the joke with the fact right under it; microcopy in full voice | Snacks, drinks, small consumer goods, playful apps | Jokes aimed at the customer, or on money and health purchases |
| Literary and formal | Exact, slightly raised vocabulary; materials by name; no exclamation marks | Skincare, fragrance, design objects | It turns ornate without being exact |
| Proper nouns and restraint | Places, buildings, history do the work; the word "luxury" never appears | Luxury hotels, heritage houses, galleries | There is no real place or history in the brief |
| Sensory facts, second person | What the reader will see, smell, hear, each a literal fact; one defining absence | Food, fitness, spas, anything used with the body | Every section turns sensory, including prices |
| Historical scene | Opens on a dated moment, not the brand | Family firms, restaurants with real lineage | The history is invented or generic |
| Plain-spoken activist | The mission as one measurable sentence; admits its own harm | Mission-led brands, non-profits | The mission is abstract nouns |
| Refusal and narrowness | Positioned by what it will not do; scarcity as an honest limit | Makers, specialists, single-product firms | The refusal is invented to sound focused |
| Product as the hero | Almost no "we"; every turn of phrase followed by a spec | Hardware, single-feature apps | A play on words has no fact under it |
| Hurried and conversational | Talks to one busy person; asides in brackets; permission to leave | Small studios, freelancers, portfolios | It gets chummy, or jokes replace information |

Trade-to-register pairings are starting points, never defaults: a category does not pick the voice any more than it picks the visual style (`frontend-design`'s category test).

## 4. Page shape from the reader's questions

A reader asks, roughly in order: what is this, is it for me, why believe you, how does it work, what does it cost, what if it goes wrong, what do I do now. Their awareness sets where the page starts:

- **Unaware of the problem:** open the sub-line on a moment they recognise.
- **Aware of the problem:** name it in the customer's words, then the product.
- **Comparing options:** lead with the mechanism, what this does differently.
- **Already know the client:** product, offer, price, action.

Then:

- **A section exists only if the brief has true material for it.** Five full sections beat eight padded ones.
- **Counts follow the content.** Four real features are four cards; one quote is one quote; zero testimonials is a normal page.
- **Look for the organising idea** (the menu as the page, a timetable, a founder's letter, one object carried through) and let it break the template once.
- **Vary what goes in sections:** a list, a table, a short letter, one longer paragraph, a heading with one line. Not eyebrow, claim, lede, three cards, figures in every section.

## 5. Moves for each part of the page

- **Hero.** Short (a handful of words), one clause, never two stacked adjectives. For a brand nobody knows yet, naming the category plus the buyer or place is the safe default ("Scheduling app for dental clinics"). A narrow superlative is allowed only when the brief proves it and the next line says how. Riddles, moods, and bare commands do not carry a hero for an unknown brand.
- **Sub-line.** How it works and the main benefit in one or two sentences, with one fact or mechanism, never a row of adjectives. A greeting, the reader's state ("Forty unread invoices since Monday?"), an enemy, or product news can open the sub-line, a section, or the close, each with a fact beside it.
- **Section headings.** Default to a name with a claim under it ("Classes and prices" / "Eight classes a week, none over twelve people"). A verb and its object works where the reader does something ("Send quotes from the job site"). A plain claim with its noun, or a figure from the brief, also works. Avoid bare commands ("Get started") and headings that only make sense after reading the page.
- **About and story.** Start in a moment the brief gives, not a thesis. Use anecdotes or a timeline, never value pillars ("Our values: quality, community, passion"). Failure and luck belong in the plot when the brief has them. State values as behaviours with the brief's own numbers ("We repair anything we sold, free, for ten years"), never invented ones. End in a modest present tense, not a vision, and say how to reach them.
- **Features.** The mechanism in verb-led steps; the outcome first with the feature as its proof; a moment rather than a use case; real values (times, sizes, amounts), never "customisable". Card length follows what the brief knows.
- **Proof.** Only what the brief gives, quoted word for word with name and role. Let others say the superlatives. A rule can replace a claim ("Every order is packed by the person who roasted it"). None is fine.
- **The plain zone (prices, FAQ, booking).** Facts only. Perks priced and literal. A risk reversal beside the button only when true. Admit limits in the FAQ. Ask the buyer's real objection and answer it flatly.
- **Buttons and the close.** A verb and the real object, literal even when the register plays (grammar → [naming-features-and-labels.md](../naming-features-and-labels.md)). Offer a low-pressure exit for browsers where the register fits ("See the menu first"). The close restates the hero's promise with one fact and repeats the main action word for word.
- **Microcopy carries the voice.** A stock form error under a hand-tuned hero gives the page away. Write form errors, the sent message, empty states, the 404, and the newsletter confirmation in the register. This is where a dry line is most welcome. Failure-copy rules still apply → [error-messages.md](../error-messages.md).
- **Footer.** Facts only: legal row, address, hours. No sign-off line and no pun on the name.
- **Numbers and humour.** When the brief gives them, odd human numbers beat round big ones. Never adjust or invent a figure to look specific. Every number sits in the sentence it proves, with its unit. Humour points at ourselves, never the customer, and lives in asides and microcopy, not at the end of every paragraph. On a serious purchase (money, health), keep it to the microcopy.
- **Repeat the product's name** instead of cycling through "the platform, the tool, the solution" (the parent's one-term rule).

## 6. Device budget

None of these is banned; each works once. Over budget is the tell. Counts are per page.

| Device | Budget |
| --- | --- |
| Two-fragment heading ("X. Y.") | 1 |
| Number-pair heading ("Four X, one Y") | 0–1 |
| Negation run ("No X. No Y.") | 1, and only when each item is a refusal the buyer can check |
| Contrast frames ("X, not Y", "rather than", "instead of") | 2 in total, counted together |
| Dry closing line on a paragraph | 2, never consecutive |
| Pun on the name | 1, never the hero |
| Footer sign-off | 0 |
| Figure strip | Only facts the brief gives |
| Virtue words about ourselves (honest, calm, genuinely) | 0 |
| Lists of three | Only when there are three things |
| Step numbers on cards | Only where order is the content |
| Response-time promise, "Since [year]" | 1 each, from the brief, worded the same everywhere |

## 7. Checks

1. **Rival-name swap.** Put a rival's name, or the same trade in another town, into the hero, the sub-line, and each heading. A line that still reads true says nothing about this client. Rewrite it from the fact sheet.
2. **Sameness across pages.** When the session wrote other pages, read their first headings side by side. A shared cadence or joke is a tic.
3. **Read it aloud as the client.** Cut every sentence they would not say about themselves (the parent's read-aloud check, from the client's mouth).
4. **Over-correction.** After fixes, look for the habit that replaced the removed one: "rather than" multiplying, every sentence a fragment, everything in pairs, a joke deleted with nothing in its place. The table lives in `frontend-design` (anti-slop-catalog).
5. **A fresh reader.** When a subagent is available, give it only the page text and ask: what is this, who is it for, why this over the alternative, what does it cost, what would make it hesitate, and which three lines sound like any other website. Fix what it finds. Never iterate to beat an AI detector.

## Conflicts with other sources

- **Headings as questions.** The source forbids question headings outside the FAQ but allows the reader's-state question as a sub-line. Vercel's writing guidelines ban rhetorical questions in docs. Keep both scoped: no rhetorical questions in product UI or docs; on a marketing page, one reader's-state question is allowed when the register supports it and a fact follows.
- **Capitalisation.** The source accepts title case when it is the client's house style. This skill's default stays sentence case; house style wins when it exists.
- **Category-naming hero.** The source makes it mandatory. Here it is the safe default for an unknown brand, because a known brand or a pinned brief can earn a different form.
- **Word-per-screen budgets** (the source sets fixed counts per page kind) were not adopted: they are one kit's house style. The parent's copy-budget rule applies instead.
