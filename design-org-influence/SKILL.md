# Design Org Influence

**Sources/gaps:** [references/sources.md](references/sources.md) — Femke corpus + Dive Club interviews + Nabors/Heath/Buley books; unresolved promotion candidates in [references/coverage-gaps.md](references/coverage-gaps.md).

## Core reframe: it's the framing, not the design

**"It's not your design they don't trust, it's how you're framing the value."** Rejection is rarely about craft, leadership, or politics — it's solution-first thinking: falling in love with a solution before understanding the business problem, presenting features instead of outcomes. The designers who consistently get ideas approved "think business first and design second."

- **Order taker → product-thinking designer.** Someone suggests a feature; most designers say "Okay, I'll design it." The influential designer asks "What problem are we solving?" / "How will we know this worked?" — "That tiny shift from order taker to problem solver changes everything for your career." (Julie Zhuo framing: [references/pm-partnership.md](references/pm-partnership.md).)
- **Waiter → problem owner** (Perri, *Escaping the Build Trap*, ch. 6). "It's not the customer's job to come up with their own solutions. That is your job." Served feature orders feed the product death cycle; move every request back to outcome, audience, current behavior, and success measure ([references/influence-field-notes.md](references/influence-field-notes.md)).
- **The pre-Figma gate.** Before anything else, write down three things: (1) What business problem are we solving? (2) What user problem are we solving? (3) How will we measure success? "If you don't have answers to those questions, don't open Figma yet" — align with your PM first. "User problems and business problems aren't always the same thing."
- **Don't delegate impact** (Soleio via Julie Zhuo, 2026). Designers who hand strategy and impact to PMs cannot ask to be treated as excellent; they must help shape the why, pressure-test whether the idea should exist, and use design to give definition to the what-if. The practical move is not turf war: ask the PM's questions yourself, then bring sharper evidence and options.
- **"Always have a pitch for your complaints"** (Steven Steiner). Never bring a problem without a proposed move. Also his door-opener: "How can I help you right now?"
- **Reposition yourself**: "not as someone who slows things down with research, but as someone who de-risks decisions." Exploratory work without metrics = frame as risk reduction ("2 weeks validating assumptions vs 3 months of wasted eng time").

## Checklist

Run before any pitch, pushback reply, or review write-up.

- [ ] Pre-Figma gate answered: business problem, user problem, success measure?
- [ ] Pitch leads with the business problem in one sentence, with numbers?
- [ ] Pitch has one Commander’s Intent, a concrete customer/team scene, and human-scale numbers?
- [ ] Three layers of evidence + implementation plan (phases, MVP, success metrics, rollback)?
- [ ] Objections anticipated — and the stakeholder's goal/motivation decoded first?
- [ ] Pushback handled as ask-why → reframe-to-goal → propose-a-test, using the conflict script?
- [ ] Roles negotiated with the PM, with a quarterly retro — not assumed?
- [ ] Wins doc current (one SBI entry/week), invisible work included?
- [ ] Manager relationship aligned explicitly: what success looks like for them, for you, and for the relationship?
- [ ] Bet ledger current: what you pushed for, lost, won, and later learned?
- [ ] Influence mode chosen deliberately: advisor for frames, solver for proof?
- [ ] Conflict reframed around shared goal + different assumptions?
- [ ] Timing window chosen or urgency manufactured?
- [ ] If solo/early UX: listening tour done, informal supporters named, and the ask framed as scope rather than permission?

## Pitching for approval

Full scripts, worked numbers, and timing windows: [references/pitching.md](references/pitching.md).

- **The impact framework — 6 steps**: (1) identify the business problem first ("Our checkout abandonment rate is 68%, costing us $2.3 million annually" — not "this checkout flow has usability issues"); (2) connect design to metrics leadership cares about; (3) present with data — three layers (quantitative foundation → qualitative insights → solution with projected impact); (4) anticipate objections, decode stakeholder motivations; (5) clear implementation plan (phased rollout, the MVP that "delivers 80% of the value"); (6) time the pitch strategically. "Your design brief should read like a business case, not a creative brief."
- **The strategic narrative framework — 4 parts**: problem context (what's broken, in business terms) → your insight → design solution (features connected to outcomes) → expected impact (quantify where you can, "be directional when you can't").
- **Four lenses for craft/design-system asks** (when you hear "opportunity cost"): customer impact · company goals · team efficiency ("That's not opportunity cost, that's opportunity creation") · tech-debt timing ("the cheapest it will ever be"). Bring engineering allies so it isn't you advocating alone.
- **Timing**: planning/budget cycles, competitor launches, bad metrics, churn spikes — or manufacture the urgency. "Smart designers don't just wait for the perfect timing, they create it."
- **Before/after**: lead with the metric drop, the projected lift, and the rollout plan, not "the current design looks outdated." Same design, completely different reception.
- **Business translation, not design translation** (Sten): stop explaining whitespace, systems, and flows; restate the decision in revenue, retention, support cost, debt, and risk. Write knowns vs assumptions before selling a concept, and pick the artifact fidelity for the conversation you need.
- **Translate per audience** (Hogan): one elevator pitch per audience, in that audience's motivations, repeated often.
- **Show the delta**: the before/after craft deck (Briggs — axes in [references/influence-field-notes.md](references/influence-field-notes.md)) and the animated-vs-static comp (Nabors — "talk benefits"; be a gardener, not a gatekeeper).

## Sticky framing (Heath & Heath)

The last pass on a stakeholder pitch or strategy memo, never a substitute for evidence. Five checks — full quotes and examples: [references/influence-field-notes.md](references/influence-field-notes.md).

- **Find the Commander’s Intent.** One pitch gets one lead — "If you say three things, you don't say anything."
- **Break the right guessing machine.** Name the current assumption, show why it fails, replace it with a better decision rule.
- **Make it concrete before asking for belief.** Lead with specific people doing specific things — a customer scene, support ticket, prototype moment.
- **Put numbers at human scale.** "$500/employee/year" → "one or two minutes a day."
- **Carry the moral in a story.** A concrete incident over a slogan.

## Handling objections

Full playbooks per phrase + escalation: [references/objections.md](references/objections.md).

- **The four PM phrases**: "I don't like this" · "this will take too long / no resources" · "it looks great, but this isn't a priority" · "I don't think it's gonna work." One response pattern: **ask why → reframe to the goal ("which of these concepts best helps us achieve that goal?") → propose a test** (usability test, A/B, phased milestone).
- **The conflict script** (framed around shared success): *"I want us to succeed at [goal]. I'm concerned about X because [reason]. What if we tried Y?"* "Conflict isn't personal. It's about the goal." Productive pushback builds trust.
- **Decode before defending** (Florian): a pushback may be about a perceived gap in your process — fix the expectation, not the mockup.
- **No project data? Cite empirical psychology** (Yablonski): borrowed evidence is weaker than your own data, far stronger than taste-versus-taste.

## The PM partnership

Full negotiation scripts, translation templates, and Craig/Julie Zhuo framings: [references/pm-partnership.md](references/pm-partnership.md).

- **Negotiate roles explicitly** (Miranda Slater): "These are what I feel like I should be responsible for. What do you feel like you should be responsible for?" — try lines for a quarter, run a retro, iterate.
- **Set the expectation early**: ask to be "approached with problems and not solutions," then co-create discovery. Your PM isn't your boss; they're your partner.
- **Empathy-first decode** (Miranda): don't conclude "the PM is just a control freak" — "It could be a past experience or lack of experience" with designers. We don't extend PMs the empathy we extend users.
- **"Think of your stakeholders as your users"** (Miranda): of 16 things you did, pull forward the four that prove strategic value, in their language, not UX jargon.
- **Invite yourself to the rooms you're left out of** (Metts & Welfle): three scripts (listen-in, productivity enabler, helping hand) plus weekly office hours. "Don't confuse making a deliverable with making a difference."

## Promotion & reviews

Full anti-patterns, SBI detail, and the review-logging workflow: [references/promotion-and-reviews.md](references/promotion-and-reviews.md).

- **Five seniority anti-patterns**: waiting for permission · outputs over outcomes · not building relationships proactively · avoiding conflict · underselling your impact. "Outputs don't get you promoted."
- **Wins doc**: one entry per week — problem, what you did, impact (qualitative counts). "No one's going to care about my career as much as I do."
- **SBI (Situation–Behavior–Impact)** — the Center for Creative Leadership's technique (per Kim Scott, *Radical Candor*: it makes feedback "more precise and therefore less arrogant") — for every entry: situation with a number → what *you* specifically did → what changed. Same work, completely different picture.
- **Four self-reflection mistakes**: responsibilities instead of contributions · outputs instead of outcomes · vague-to-seem-humble ("Vague is not humble, just unhelpful") · only writing about wins (all-sunshine "reads as unaware").
- **Invisible work** — "There's no Figma file for prevented a bad decision": log the reversed decision, the aligning brief, the 45-minute unblock when it happens or it's gone.
- **The credibility ladder** (Miranda Slater): master the language → find small wins → build credibility gradually → then ask for your seat at the table.
- **Log continuously, not at the deadline**: Femke's stateful Claude-skill review logger (setup interview → artifacts converted to SBI entries tagged to competencies → attributed peer-feedback log → end-of-period synthesis) is the reusable architecture.

## Career as a business case (Ryan Scott)

Designers are hired for craft but promoted for business value — treat that gap as the career strategy. Sell to budget-holders, not design managers; describe work as driving a named metric in a direction, never as "a redesign"; cite many kinds of value (competitor neutralization, velocity, quality, brand) so no single number carries the pitch; float the bare idea with allies first and never skip levels; weigh Value–Cost–Risk rather than RICE; ask for the analytics tool yourself. Read [references/influence-field-notes.md](references/influence-field-notes.md) (Career as a business case) for the nine rules with quotes and receipts — overfiltering and role-convergence game theory included — before writing a portfolio, promo case, or results slide.

## Managing your manager

Julie Zhuo (Looking Glass, 2024): repairing the manager-report contract from the report side.

- **Drop the executioner/white-knight model.** Fear says the manager is waiting to punish mistakes; pining says the manager should defend and promote you. Both make the manager the main character in your career. Recenter on your own agency.
- **Define the relationship you want.** Before judging the manager, write what a great manager-report relationship would look like, then describe that aspiration in a 1:1 and ask whether they agree.
- **Make the ask specific.** "More support" is too vague. Ask for concrete help: more context on a decision, sharper feedback on a project, clearer promotion expectations, earlier visibility into priorities.
- **Align on both jobs.** Ask: "How do you think about your role? What makes you feel you're doing a great job?" Then ask the mirror for yourself: "What would make you feel I'm doing a great job?" Trust improves when both success definitions are explicit.
- **Run a monthly feedback loop.** Tell them what is helping, ask for more of what you need, and ask them for feedback. If strong repeated feedback is ignored, escalate to the manager's manager; if the chain loses trust, leave rather than normalizing mediocre management.

## Influence modes, politics, and culture change (Zhuo)

- **Know what they care about in <50 words.** If you cannot answer "What does X care about?" and "Can my proposal genuinely help with that?", you are not ready to pitch. Ask first; then route your proposal through their goal.
- **Keep a bet ledger.** Track what you pushed for, where you disagreed, what got overruled, what later happened, and what you learned. Good accounting sharpens product judgment and builds promotion evidence without relying on memory.
- **Advisor vs solver mode.** Use advisor mode when the goal is to change someone's decision frame: words, stories, principles, alternatives. Use solver mode when the goal is to make a specific decision obvious: prototypes, experiments, narrowed pathways. As a report influencing a manager, default to solver mode; reduce decision burden.
- **Reframe "politics" as assumptions.** In competent organizations, most politics is not villainy; it is hidden shared goals, different assumptions, and bad translation. Start conflict by asking which position is most likely to help the company win and what unique knowledge each side has.
- **Change culture by changing beliefs.** Identify the current belief behind the behavior, why reasonable people adopted it, where it fails, and the replacement belief that serves the same goal better. Then repeat the new story until someone else says it.

## Build-trap diagnosis (Perri / Bungay)

When the org keeps asking for more plans, controls, or features instead of better outcomes, name the build trap: success measured by outputs rather than outcomes. Diagnose which of Bungay's three gaps (Knowledge, Alignment, Effects) is being patched with more reporting, instruction, or controls; the corrective is strategic intent — leadership sets goals and boundaries, teams decide how, evidence closes the loop. Autonomy without strategy is paralysis. Gap detail, quotes, and Perri's six leadership-room diagnostic questions: [references/influence-field-notes.md](references/influence-field-notes.md).

## Team-of-one influence (Buley)

For the only UX/design person on a team, or an org too immature for formal process (method catalog routes to `user-research` and `design-prototyping`). Full quotes: [references/influence-field-notes.md](references/influence-field-notes.md).

- **Principles over process.** Pushing process on an immature org is a distraction — "invite non-UX people into your process. Listen with genuine curiosity. Make ideas tangible."
- **Good enough is an influence tool.** Imperfect artifacts invite participation; polished proof can wait.
- **Run a Listening Tour before the big pitch**, and share what you heard before prescribing.
- **Negotiate size, not permission.** Offer a large study vs. a small informal evaluation — how much UX, not whether.
- **Build the pyramid, not the executive-only pitch.** Supporters throughout the org; the lunch date is the durable unit of influence.

## Relationship to other skills

- **`people-management`** — running your own team of direct reports (1:1s, feedback, careers, hiring/firing process, team comms); this skill is influence up/sideways and your own promotion.
- **`personal-growth`** — broader reflection on the user's own life/work fit, goals, recovery, and whether to persist or change direction; this skill owns promotion, managing a manager, and stakeholder influence inside the current organization.
- **`product-decision-making`** — whether the proposal is well-framed: user problem, data, success measure, optimization vs new behavior, and owner-by-outcome. This skill translates that judgment into buy-in and org movement.
- **`user-research`** — runs the studies whose findings power your business case; its research-as-leadership tactics feed this skill's credibility ladder.
- **`design-prototyping`** — owns session mechanics and pitch presentation craft (how to structure a review, fidelity, demos); this skill owns the business case and political context around the pitch.
- **`building-in-public`** — influence with an external audience; this skill is influence inside the org.
- **`design-craft`** — defines the quality bar; this skill gets the org to fund it.
- **`design-systems`** — DS governance/adoption mechanics; this skill supplies the four-lens business case for DS investment.
- **`write-clear-prose`** — the writing layer for briefs, self-reviews, and pitch docs.
